Why It Matters

❦ ❦ ❦

Clear communication shouldn't be a privilege.

What is health literacy?

The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services defines personal health literacy as the degree to which individuals have the ability to find, understand, and use information and services to inform health-related decisions and actions for themselves and others. Its counterpart, organizational health literacy, is the degree to which organizations equitably enable people to do exactly that.

That second half matters. Health literacy is not only a skill a patient does or doesn't have — it is also a measure of how well the system explains itself.

The barrier of medical language

Medical terminology is dense, intimidating, and rarely explained. A patient reading their own lab results or discharge summary can find themselves locked out of a document written about their own body. Thousands of patients and their families across San Diego navigate this every day.

Language is rarely the only obstacle. It compounds with every other barrier to care — insurance, transportation, time off work — and lands hardest on patients with limited English proficiency or lower literacy levels, who are already the least likely to have the others cleared for them.

Youth carry a particular share of it. Many are developing their own literacy skills and health habits while simultaneously acting as linguistic and cultural mediators for their relatives — translating appointments, forms, and diagnoses for the people they love (Santos, 2018).

What low health literacy costs

According to Berkman et al. (2011), low health literacy:

  • Delays care
  • Reduces treatment adherence
  • Increases hospitalizations and use of emergency care
  • Erodes trust between patients and providers

Healthcare systems are working toward plain-language communication, but that change is slow and uneven (Koh & Rudd, 2015). In the meantime, people still have to read their own charts.

Why the classics?

The same few hundred Greek and Latin parts recombine to form tens of thousands of medical words. Learn the parts once, and an entire vocabulary becomes decodable — rote memorization turns into pattern recognition.

This isn't only intuition. Two studies suggest the root-scaffolding approach works:

  • Keen et al. (2025) found that, for college students, enrollment in the Classics morphemes course at UC Davis before or alongside biology and anatomy courses was positively associated with higher grade points.
  • McAllister et al. (2022) found that, for nursing associate students, a short online intervention on decoding medical terminology through roots, prefixes, and suffixes was associated with significantly higher understanding — a 20% mean increase in test scores.

Our vision

A world where understanding the language of medicine is not a privilege but a shared skill. Low health literacy affects millions of patients and drives worse outcomes, and everyone has a role to play in closing that gap. Step by step, we believe the health literacy crisis in San Diego and beyond is solvable.
